Complexity

Studying the consequences of simple messages in politics

This project speaks to multiple strands of political communication and behavior research, including the mass–elite linkage literature on how parties convey their positions to voters (Adams 2001; Budge et al. 2001; Laver et al. 2003), the growing body of work on manifesto analysis and quantitative text methods (Slapin & Proksch 2008; Spirling 2015), and studies of populist communication strategies emphasizing clarity and directness (Canovan 1999; Moffitt & Tormey 2014).

While much research examines what parties say (positional shifts, issue emphasis), very little has focused on how they say it—specifically, the role of linguistic complexity in shaping voter knowledge and perceptions. Prior work has largely overlooked variation in the readability of campaign messages and its downstream effects on political cognition.

How we addressed it

  • Textual analysis: We developed and validated the LIX readability index (Björnsson 1968) to measure complexity in 175 party manifestos from Austria and Germany (1945–2013).
  • Survey experiment: We conducted a preregistered two‑wave vignette experiment and a conjoint experiment in Germany (N = 5,800) testing whether simple versus sophisticated message framing causally affects political knowledge and candidate perceptions.

Key Findings

  1. Populist simplicity: Populist parties systematically use less complex language in their manifestos—shorter words, shorter sentences—than mainstream competitors.
  2. Improved ideological placement: Voters are more accurate in locating parties on the left–right spectrum when parties’ campaign messages are simpler.
  3. Boosted comprehension: Simple messages significantly improve citizens’ understanding of policy positions.
  4. Heuristic effects: Simple message framing leads citizens to perceive speakers as more “of the people” rather than part of an elite.

Implications

For practice: While simplifying campaign language can democratize information processing and foster broader engagement, practitioners must balance clarity with nuance. Overly simplistic messages risk reducing complex policy debates to soundbites, potentially misleading voters and eroding informed democratic deliberation.

For social science: This research highlights linguistic style as a crucial dimension of political communication. It calls for integrating readability metrics into discourse analyses, but also for critical examination of when simplicity becomes an oversimplification that undermines democratic discourse. Future work should explore the threshold at which message clarity shifts from enabling understanding to obscuring complexity.
