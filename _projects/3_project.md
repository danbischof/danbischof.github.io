  <h2>Complexity in Language</h2>

  <h3>Literature</h3>
  <p>This project speaks to multiple strands of political communication and behavior research, including the mass–elite linkage literature on how parties convey their positions to voters (Adams 2001; Budge et al. 2001; Laver et al. 2003), the growing body of work on manifesto analysis and quantitative text methods (Slapin &amp; Proksch 2008; Spirling 2015), and studies of populist communication strategies emphasizing clarity and directness (Canovan 1999; Moffitt &amp; Tormey 2014).</p>

  <h3>Gap in the literature</h3>
  <p>While much research examines <strong>what</strong> parties say (positional shifts, issue emphasis), very little has focused on <strong>how</strong> they say it—specifically, the role of linguistic complexity in shaping voter knowledge and perceptions. Prior work has largely overlooked variation in the readability of campaign messages and its downstream effects on political cognition.</p>

  <h3>How I addressed it</h3>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Textual analysis:</strong> I developed and validated the LIX readability index (Björnsson 1968) to measure complexity in 175 party manifestos from Austria and Germany (1945–2013).</li>
    <li><strong>Survey experiment:</strong> I conducted a preregistered three‑wave vignette experiment in Germany (N = 5,800) testing whether simple versus sophisticated message framing causally affects political knowledge and candidate perceptions.</li>
  </ul>

  <h3>Key Findings</h3>
  <ol>
    <li><strong>Populist simplicity:</strong> Populist parties systematically use less complex language in their manifestos—shorter words, shorter sentences—than mainstream competitors.</li>
    <li><strong>Improved ideological placement:</strong> Voters are more accurate in locating parties on the left–right spectrum when parties’ campaign messages are simpler.</li>
    <li><strong>Boosted comprehension:</strong> Simple messages significantly improve citizens’ understanding of policy positions.</li>
    <li><strong>Heuristic effects:</strong> Simple message framing leads citizens to perceive speakers as more “of the people” rather than part of an elite.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>Implications</h3>
  <p><strong>For practice:</strong> Simplifying campaign language can democratize information processing, foster broader engagement, and pressure mainstream parties to adopt clearer messaging for electoral competitiveness. Media coverage may likewise gravitate toward more digestible, simplified messages.</p>
  <p><strong>For social science:</strong> This research underlines the importance of linguistic style as a core dimension of political communication. It advocates integrating readability metrics into manifesto and discourse analyses and demonstrates the value of experimental methods to establish causal effects of message simplicity on voter cognition and perceptions.</p>
